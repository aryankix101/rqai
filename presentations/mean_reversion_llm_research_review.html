What the two results actually mean

  • Visible V2: ticker identity improved the coarse top-30 cutoff, but score order inside that group was not reliably monotonic.
  • Anonymized V2: the cutoff result was weaker, but relative rank carried more information when higher ranks received more weight.
  • Percentiles: both equal-weight selections beat most random alternatives; 99.7 versus 96.2 is not proof that visibility improves ranking.
  • Score magnitude: raw-score sizing produced 14.33%—essentially no CAGR improvement over equal weighting.
Production implication: use anonymized scores to choose the top 30, but keep those names equally weighted. The development ranking result is informative—not sufficient evidence to deploy rank concentration.

Why the headline winners were rejected

  • Exponential weighting: the 2009–2014 lift reversed in 2015–2019; the weighted book lost money while equal 30/30 stayed positive.
  • Long concentration: 15 longs looked much better in development, but finished slightly below 30 longs in validation.
  • Short concentration: 10 shorts added 1.20pp in development, then trailed the 30-short book by 1.74pp in validation.
  • Extreme top-10/h1: 34.77% was a search-selected development headline; validation collapsed to −13.95% with a −72.17% drawdown.
  • Raw-score weighting: 14.33% versus 14.33% equal weight gave no development CAGR case for cardinal sizing.
Decision: retain equal-weight 30/30 and the 50/50 long–short split. Use scores for selection only; reject exponential weighting, score-proportional sizing, and concentrated shorts.

Side asymmetry

Development attribution favored longs—but it did not justify concentrated production weights

+4.85pp
Long-selection lift

In 2009–2014, replacing Trail5 with anonymized V2 on longs raised CAGR from 12.01% to 16.86% under the same h10 wrapper.

+2.06pp
Short-selection lift

Replacing only the short ranking raised development CAGR from 12.01% to 14.07% under that wrapper.

+7.01pp
Joint development lift

Using V2 on both sides reached 19.02% versus 12.01% for Trail5—but this wrapper later lost money in validation.

What visible versus anonymized actually showed

  • Equal-weight top 30: visible V2 led anonymized V2, 16.22% to 14.33%.
  • Rank-decay h10: anonymized V2 led visible V2, 19.05% to 13.98%.
  • The visible scores were better at the coarse top-30 cutoff; the anonymized scores had the stronger ordering inside the pool.
  • These are development diagnostics. The later failure of rank weighting overrides the attractive 19.05% headline.

Why we did not concentrate the short book

  • Research searched six short-concentration settings and then retained the best-looking one.
  • After accounting for that search, randomized rankings produced an equally strong or stronger winner 7.35% of the time—not below the conventional 5% threshold.
  • More importantly, validation was worse: 10 shorts returned 0.88% CAGR versus 2.62% for the diversified 30-short book.
  • The short-ranking signal was therefore suggestive in development, not robust enough to size aggressively.
Decision: keep 30 equal-weight shorts. If a future asymmetric design is tested, start with the long sleeve and require an untouched-period confirmation before changing production weights.
Sources: autoresearch_v2_cache_sweep/anonymized_exponential_2x2_isolation.csv; short_rank_concentration_search_null.csv; phase2_validation_tracker/cross_period_autoresearch_detail.csv

2015–2019 validation

Explaining the input features helped the diversified 30/30 book—but not every construction

What “glossary” means: a section of the prompt that defines every supplied feature and its scale—for example, Trail5 is the trailing five-day return, RSI5 is five-day RSI, and Rng55 is the stock’s position within its 55-day range. Both variants received the feature values; only the glossary variant received these explanations.

1.56%
Deterministic Trail5 control

No LLM; equal-weight 30 longs and 30 shorts.

0.90%
V2 without feature explanations

Underperformed Trail5 by 0.66pp.

2.62%
V2 with feature explanations

Beat Trail5 by 1.06pp and no-glossary V2 by 1.72pp.

What the clean 30/30 comparison showed

  • Adding feature definitions raised V2 CAGR from 0.90% to 2.62% with dates, pools, accounting, and portfolio construction held fixed.
  • Glossary V2 also beat the 1.56% Trail5 control.
  • Versus Trail5, Sharpe improved from 0.203 to 0.304 and max drawdown improved from −15.07% to −13.15%.

Why this is not “the glossary always helps”

  • When the same scores were used in six concentrated or aggressively weighted portfolios, no-glossary V2 was stronger in five.
  • The benefit therefore interacted with how names were selected and weighted; it was not a universal improvement in every portfolio.
  • Those concentrated wrappers were themselves unstable, so they do not overturn the clean 30/30 result.
Decision: retain the feature glossary for the production equal-weight 30/30 book. Treat its benefit as specific to that tested construction—not as permission to deploy concentrated weighting.
Source: phase2_validation_tracker/phase2_scorecard.csv, Validation 2015–2019 glossary and no-glossary rows

Appendix · prompt and schema contract

The LLM ranks candidates; Python validates the response and builds the portfolio

The model is one controlled ranking step inside a deterministic pipeline. It cannot add securities, switch their side, choose the final book, or set position weights.

1. Code supplies
60 long + 60 short candidates
2. Prompt explains
features and scoring task
3. LLM returns
one score for all 120 names
4. Code validates and selects
top 30 per side
5. Code constructs
equal-weight 50/50 book

What the LLM is allowed to return

long_scores: 60 × { supplied long ID, score 0–100 } short_scores: 60 × { supplied short ID, score 0–100 }
  • Every supplied identifier appears exactly once on its original side.
  • Scores express relative ordering within the long or short candidate pool.
  • No rationales, labels, security additions, selections, weights, or commentary.

What deterministic Python controls

  • Reject malformed JSON, missing or duplicate IDs, wrong-side names, and scores outside 0–100.
  • Sort each side by score and select exactly 30 longs and 30 shorts.
  • Assign equal weights and maintain the fixed 50/50 long–short capital split.
  • Only a validated response can enter the cache and backtest.
Concrete safety example: the temperature-1.5 run returned a score of 800. Validation rejected that response, so it could not affect portfolio selection or reported performance.
Source: mean_reversion_llm_v2.py V2_SYSTEM_PROMPT and V2_MINIMAL_CHANGE_CONTROL_PROMPT; mean_reversion_llm_v1.py validate_llm_scores
